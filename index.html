Basic Course Information

Course Description:

How can we use data to understand complex systems, evaluate relationships, make predictions, and support better decisions? How do we determine which patterns in data are meaningful, which models are appropriate, and how much confidence we should place in their results?

This course introduces the foundations of statistical modeling and machine learning. We examine the full modeling process: exploring and preparing data, formulating questions and hypotheses, constructing models, evaluating assumptions and performance, comparing alternative models, and translating results into meaningful conclusions and actionable recommendations.

Topics include data visualization and exploration, statistical inference, linear and generalized linear models, analysis of variance and covariance, model diagnostics and selection, missing data, dimensionality reduction, classification, and prediction. The course considers both deductive approaches, in which models are motivated by hypotheses and knowledge about a system, and inductive approaches, in which models and patterns are learned from data. This progression provides a bridge from classical statistical modeling to modern machine learning.

Students use R to analyze real datasets, build and evaluate models, and communicate results through reproducible analyses. The course provides a foundation for more advanced work in data mining and data-driven systems engineering.

Learning Objectives:
  • Apply statistical modeling to understand complex systems and support evidence-informed engineering decisions
  • Explore, visualize, and assess data to identify patterns, limitations, and potential sources of bias
  • Formulate meaningful, testable questions and hypotheses from real-world problems and associated data
  • Select and apply appropriate modeling techniques for inference, prediction, and classification
  • Build and interpret linear and generalized linear models using R
  • Evaluate model assumptions, diagnostics, uncertainty, and performance
  • Identify and address issues including missing data, outliers, influential observations, overfitting, and model generalization
  • Compare alternative models and explain tradeoffs between explanatory and predictive modeling approaches
  • Apply machine learning methods and evaluate their performance and generalization ability
  • Develop actionable recommendations supported by statistical evidence while clearly communicating uncertainty and limitations
  • Communicate data analyses, modeling decisions, and results through reproducible analyses and technical reports
Prerequisites:

It is recommended that students have a basic command of linear algebra, calculus, probability, statistics and programming experience.

Course Resources

There is no required textbook for this course. The following open-access texts and references will support course topics in statistical modeling, machine learning, and R.

  • An Introduction to Statistical Learning by Gareth James, Daniela Witten, Trevor Hastie, Robert Tibshirani, and Jonathan Taylor. An accessible introduction to statistical modeling, prediction, classification, and machine learning using R.
  • The Elements of Statistical Learning by Trevor Hastie, Robert Tibshirani, and Jerome Friedman. A more advanced reference on statistical learning and machine learning.
  • Linear Models with R by Julian Faraway. A reference for linear models, inference, diagnostics, and modeling in R.
  • Applied Linear Statistical Models by Michael Kutner, Christopher Nachtsheim, John Neter, and William Li. A comprehensive reference for regression, analysis of variance, and related statistical modeling methods.
  • R for Data Science (2e) by Hadley Wickham, Mine Çetinkaya-Rundel, and Garrett Grolemund. A practical reference for working with, visualizing, transforming, and communicating data using R.

We will use R and RStudio throughout the course for data analysis, visualization, statistical modeling, and machine learning.

Labs

Labs provide open-ended, hands-on opportunities to apply the statistical modeling concepts introduced in the course to real data. Labs emphasize the full modeling process, including data exploration and preparation, model development, evaluation and comparison, interpretation of results, and communication of conclusions and limitations.

Labs require the use of R and will generally be completed as reproducible computational analyses. Specific format and submission requirements will be provided for each lab. Labs are individual assignments, although you are encouraged to discuss course concepts, approaches, and resources with your classmates.

Unless otherwise specified, labs are open-resource, including course materials, documentation, online resources, and generative AI tools. Any use of generative AI must be documented and credited as described in the course Generative AI policy. You are responsible for understanding, evaluating, and being able to explain all code, analyses, and interpretations that you submit.

DataCamp

DataCamp provides structured practice with R, statistical modeling, and machine learning. Students will complete at least 20,000 XP over the semester from the approved DataCamp courses listed on the course website.

The approved courses span topics including R and data fundamentals, visualization, statistical inference, regression and statistical modeling, missing data, and supervised and unsupervised machine learning. Students may choose among the listed courses based on their prior experience and areas where they would most benefit from additional practice.

Students who have previously completed one of the listed DataCamp courses should select another approved course rather than repeat material for credit. Progress toward the 20,000 XP requirement will be based on completed XP from the approved course list.

In-Class Activities

In-class activities provide opportunities to practice statistical reasoning, model interpretation, computational methods, and other course concepts. Activities may include Poll Everywhere questions, short analyses, interpretation of statistical output, model evaluation, and individual or small-group problem-solving exercises.

These activities are generally graded for participation and completion rather than correctness. Because they are designed to support active participation during class, they generally cannot be made up. Additional details regarding participation and allowable missed activities will be provided on the course website.

Midterm Exam

The midterm exam assesses conceptual understanding and the ability to apply statistical modeling principles to data and analytical problems covered through multiple linear regression.

The exam may include a closed-resource component with conceptual, interpretation, and short-answer questions and an open-resource analytical component involving statistical or computational problem solving. The resources permitted for each portion of the exam will be clearly specified in advance.

Final Exam

The final exam assesses students' ability to integrate and apply concepts from across the course, including statistical modeling, model evaluation, and foundational machine learning concepts and methods.

Like the midterm, the final may include both closed-resource conceptual and open-resource analytical components. The resources permitted for each portion will be clearly specified in advance.

Unless explicitly permitted, generative AI tools and AI-enabled assistants may not be used on either exam, including open-resource portions. Review materials and example questions will be provided before each exam.

The Use of Generative AI:

Generative artificial intelligence tools—software that creates new text, images, computer code, audio, video, and other content—have become widely available. Well-known examples include ChatGPT for text and DALL•E for images. This policy governs all such tools, including those released during our semester together. You may use generative AI tools on assignments in this course when I explicitly permit you to do so. Otherwise, you should refrain from using such tools.

If you do use generative AI tools on assignments in this class, you must properly document and credit the tools themselves. Cite the tool you used, following the pattern for computer software given in the specified style guide. Additionally, please include a brief description of how you used the tool. If you choose to use generative AI tools, please remember that they are typically trained on limited datasets that may be out of date. Additionally, generative AI datasets are trained on pre-existing material, including copyrighted material; therefore, relying on a generative AI tool may result in plagiarism or copyright violations.

Finally, keep in mind that the goal of generative AI tools is to produce content that seems to have been produced by a human, not to produce accurate or reliable content; therefore, relying on a generative AI tool may result in your submission of inaccurate content. It is your responsibility—not the tool’s—to assure the quality, integrity, and accuracy of work you submit in any college course. If you use generative AI tools to complete assignments in this course, in ways that I have not explicitly authorized, I reserve the right to apply the Honor Code to your specific case. In addition, you must be wary of unintentional plagiarism or fabrication of data. Please act with integrity, for the sake of both your personal character and your academic record.

In this course, the following policy dictates the acceptable use of generative AI tools, such as ChatGPT and other similar technologies. This is a programming course and while I recognize that generative AI can be a tremendous learning tool, it is my expectation that these technologies do not impede you learning the programming concepts taught in class. However, these technologies can be used to enhance your learning experience much like one would use Stack Overflow on assignments with the exception of on exams and lab quizzes which must be utilized without generative AI.
